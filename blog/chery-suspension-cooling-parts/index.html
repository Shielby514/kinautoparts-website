Published 11 November 2026 · KINP editorial team · Procurement information

On a Chery working in a hot market, the part that fails first is rarely the one the buyer expected to order. A compressor that goes quiet at midday, an expansion tank that weeps after a long uphill run, a wheel bearing that hums at 80 km/h — each of these is reported as a single broken component, and each is usually a symptom of a system boundary that was never checked. The catalogue lists these items under Cooling System and Suspension & Steering, but the reference on the box describes a component, not the condition that destroyed it. This guide is written for the buyer who wants to source correctly: read the system first, identify the exact reference second, and only then discuss price.

The distinction matters because Chery's export range spans several engine families and more than one generation of running gear. A reference that is correct for a Tiggo built for a temperate market can be wrong for the same badge assembled for a Gulf or West African destination. Two compressor assemblies can look identical in a supplier photograph while carrying different connector keying or clutch gaps. The discipline below is the one we use when a customer sends a photo and asks us to quote — and it is deliberately conservative, because a wrong cooling part does not just fail, it can take the engine with it.

Read the system before you read the part: two loops, two failure logics

Before matching any number, decide which loop you are dealing with. Chery passenger cars carry two separate fluid circuits that buyers routinely confuse: the engine coolant loop (radiator, water pump, thermostat, expansion tank, heater core, hoses) and the air-conditioning refrigerant loop (compressor, condenser, evaporator, expansion device, receiver). They share a front-end package and a fan shroud, which is why a single photograph of a crowded engine bay is a poor basis for an enquiry. A cooling complaint and an A/C complaint travel through different physics, use different test methods, and end in different parts.

The coolant loop is a closed, liquid-filled circuit running near atmospheric pressure when cold and roughly 0.9 to 1.1 bar when hot. Its failure mode is loss of containment or loss of flow: a cracked expansion tank, a weeping water-pump seal, an air lock after a refill. The refrigerant loop is a pressurised gas-and-liquid circuit, and its failure mode is loss of charge or loss of compression: a leaking condenser, a slipping compressor clutch, a blocked evaporator. You cannot diagnose the second with the tests of the first. Treat the symptom table below as a routing tool — it tells you which loop to open, not which box to tick.

Buyers sourcing Chery parts often group suspension and cooling in one RFQ, and the two systems fail for opposing reasons: cooling parts die from heat and pressure cycles, suspension parts die from load cycles and road shock. A single supplier photograph cannot prove either condition. Record which system the complaint belongs to before you send the enquiry, and you remove half the ambiguity in one step.

How to read a Chery reference without inventing meaning

Chery references are not uniform. Some are built as a letter prefix followed by a numeric block, as in M118103010; others carry a three-letter ending such as AA or BA, as in 301000058AA and J428105010BA; a few use a longer alphanumeric string such as J524BS3401430BB. The honest reading is structural rather than poetic: the leading segment groups a family, the middle segment usually identifies the component and application, and the trailing segment distinguishes a variant, batch or market. What the trailing segment specifically encodes is not published in a way that lets us guarantee interchangeability, so we never expand one reference into another on the strength of the prefix alone.

For a buyer, this produces three practical rules. First, copy the number exactly — letters, zeros and suffix included — from the label or the purchasing record, not from memory. Second, if two references appear for one requested item, keep both in the enquiry and ask for review; do not select the cheaper or the shorter one. Third, treat any reference you cannot tie to a label or a VIN as unconfirmed. Where a supplier proposes a cross-reference, record it in its own column with the evidence offered: dimensional match, identical mounting points, the same connector keying and a stated specification. An unreadable label is not permission to guess.

When you cannot read the original number at all, the fallback data set is specific and small: the vehicle brand and model group, production year, engine code, market of destination, and a photograph of the part in position with its connector and pipe stubs visible. That set lets a supplier narrow the family. It does not let anyone confirm fitment — that still requires the VIN or the label. We write "confirm against VIN/label before ordering" on every cooling quote for this reason, and we ask buyers to repeat it back on the purchase order.

Tropical duty: why the same reference fails sooner in some markets

A cooling component's service life depends less on the badge than on the climate and road surface it works in. In Gulf states and Saudi Arabia, ambient temperatures above 45 °C push a sealed refrigerant circuit toward its pressure limit; a condenser with a marginal core and a clutch with a wide gap starts short-cycling, and an expansion tank seam that survived a European winter opens after one summer. Dust compounds it — a dust-loaded condenser face raises condensing pressure, which raises compressor load, which accelerates the next failure. The result is a fleet that consumes compressors and tanks on a schedule no temperate-market catalogue predicts.

West and East Africa add a different load. Rough roads work every suspension joint harder per kilometre than a highway car sees in ten, and the same vibration loosens hose clamps and cracks tank necks. Central Asia brings cold embrittlement: a tank or hose already heat-cycled in summer splits in winter, and the failure arrives as a sudden loss of coolant rather than a slow seep. These are not reasons to change the reference; they are reasons to state the operating condition in the RFQ so the supplier can comment on material and specification. A buyer who writes only "condenser, Tiggo" has given no clue which failure they are trying to prevent.

Bench and fitment checks before you approve an order

Cooling and A/C parts are frequently replaced on suspicion, which is how correct components get sold into the wrong cars. A short, repeatable check routine removes most of that waste. It is not a repair procedure and it does not authorise anyone to work on a pressurised system without training; it is the identification discipline a buyer should apply before the purchase order goes out.

  1. Establish the vehicle, not the badge. Record the VIN, engine code, production date and destination market. A model name alone is not an identification basis.
  2. Read the old part's label while it is still fitted. Photograph the full reference, the connector face, the pipe or hose stubs and the mounting points. One wide shot plus one close shot of the label is enough.
  3. Classify the symptom into one loop. Use the routing table above. If the complaint is warm air at idle, do not order a radiator because the engine temperature gauge also moved.
  4. Measure what can be measured. For a compressor, check clutch air gap and connector pin layout; for a condenser, compare core thickness and port side; for a tank, verify neck position and cap rating. Record the numbers beside the reference.
  5. Keep proposed alternatives separate. If a supplier offers a different reference, log it as a proposal with the supporting evidence and who reviewed it. It does not enter the accepted list until the specification is confirmed in writing.
  6. Confirm fitment at the boundary. Before payment, restate that fitment must be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering, and ask the buyer to acknowledge it. This protects both sides when a mid-cycle variant appears.

The discipline is deliberately boring. It produces a short file per enquiry — VIN, label photo, measurement notes, accepted reference, separate proposals — and that file is what makes a returned part resolvable instead of argued about.

Ordering, packing and indicative pricing

Once identity is settled, the commercial fields are the same ones that matter on any export parts order: quantity basis, specification, packing, lead time, freight to the named port, warranty terms and how long the quote remains valid. For cooling components there is one extra packing requirement — condensers, evaporators and expansion tanks are bulky and fragile relative to their unit value, so they travel best consolidated with other parts rather than as a lone carton. Compressors need the pulley and clutch protected from knock; a bent pulley on an otherwise correct part is a avoidable claim.

Questions buyers ask before a Chery cooling order

Can I order a compressor by model name alone?

No. A model name covers several compressor families with different clutch types and connectors. Send the complete reference from the old unit's label, or the VIN and a label photo. If neither is available, ask for identification review and keep the line marked unconfirmed.

Are the references in this list interchangeable within a family?

Not automatically. A family groups similar components, but suffix changes can mark a different connector, port side or production batch. Fitment must be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering. Treat a neighbouring number as a separate line, not a substitute.

Why does the same reference fail quickly in one market and slowly in another?

Because the operating profile differs. Heat, dust and road shock drive different failure mechanisms even on the same part. State the market and duty cycle in the RFQ so the supplier can comment on the stated specification; do not infer service life from a catalogue page.

Do you guarantee that a listed reference fits my vehicle?

No. A catalogue entry organises an enquiry; it does not confirm application. We ask for the VIN or the part label and confirm against it before the order is accepted. Any fitment statement must be checked against VIN/label before ordering.

Can you supply a reference that is not in the catalogue?

Yes, subject to identification. Send the VIN or a clear label photograph and we will review the reference and quote it separately. The item stays unconfirmed until the label or VIN check is complete.

What should I attach to speed up a cooling enquiry?

One wide photograph of the part in position, one close photograph of the label, the VIN, the engine code and the destination market. Add the symptom in one sentence. That set answers most of what a supplier needs before a specification can be stated.
