When Supplier Photos and OE Numbers Disagree: A Chinese-Vehicle Parts Checklist

Published 22 November 2026 · Author: Shirley | Senior Autoparts Procurement Specialist

If an enquiry reference, a packaging label and a supplied photograph disagree, keep the line unresolved. Retain the original complete number, identify the specific conflict and request a readable identification view tied to the same item. Do not choose a substitute from visual similarity.

A supplier sends three photographs and a quotation. The enquiry reference is XXXX-XXXXXX, but the marking on the part reads something close and not identical, and the packaging label shows a third number again. It is tempting to pick the version that matches the quote and move on. That is precisely the moment a parts buyer should stop, because a disagreement between an enquiry number, a physical marking and a photograph is information — it usually means the supplier has combined images from more than one item, or has quoted a neighbouring reference under the requested one. The only safe response is to keep the line unresolved until the identity is settled.

This guide is a workflow for that situation. It treats each image as evidence with a limited scope, gives you a way to log conflicts without guessing, and ends with a resend request you can copy into an email or WhatsApp message. It applies to Chinese-brand vehicle parts, where a single model name can cover several variants and where a supplier's photograph is often the only identification material a buyer receives. The goal is not to catch anyone out. It is to make sure the item on the purchase order is the item that was examined.

Three image types, three different things they prove

Most conflicts arise because a buyer treats "a photograph" as one kind of evidence. It is not. A complete-item image, a physical marking and a packaging label each prove a different thing, and none of them proves application to a vehicle on its own. Once you assign each image its proper scope, most apparent contradictions turn out to be a filing problem rather than a parts problem — two pictures of two different items placed in one folder.

The practical rule that follows is simple: never associate a close-up with a wide shot unless you can see they show the same item. Check the background, the surface, the connector position and any distinctive mark. If two images were taken on different benches, under different light, or show different levels of dust and wear, treat them as separate items until the supplier confirms otherwise.

Where conflicts usually come from

In our experience, a three-way disagreement between enquiry number, marking and packaging label has a short list of causes. Recognising the cause tells you which question to ask, instead of sending a generic "please confirm" that produces another ambiguous answer. The most common cause is a combined photograph set: a supplier keeps a library of images for a family of parts and attaches a generic set to several quotations. The second is a superseded reference, where a supplier quotes the newer number while the customer asked for the older one. The third is a genuine substitution that was never disclosed.

A fourth cause is simply a poor photograph — a label photographed at an angle, with a reflection over the last two characters, so that a reader guesses the ending. A guessed character is not a reading, and it should be recorded as unreadable rather than silently completed. The fifth cause is a market variant: the same component family carries different suffixes for different destinations, and a supplier in one market photographs a unit intended for another. None of these causes is resolved by choosing the number that looks most plausible. Each is resolved by asking for a specific additional view.

Build a conflict log, not a guess

When a discrepancy appears, create a short log beside the enquiry line. The log records what each source actually said, who supplied it and how legible it was, then assigns a conflict type and an action. It does not attempt to decide which source is correct — that decision comes after the resend. The table below is the classification we use; it keeps the line open without letting it drift into a decision.

The status column matters as much as the action column. "Unresolved" means the line stays out of the accepted list. "Blocked" means no commercial discussion until identity is restated. "Under review" means two lines coexist and neither is approved. These labels make it impossible to approve a conflicting item by accident — which is the entire purpose of the log.

How to compare a photo pair without guessing

When a supplier sends two photographs that are supposed to show the same item, compare them in a fixed order. Start with the component type: does one image show a pipe where the other shows a housing, or a motor where the other shows a core? Then compare the connector arrangement — pin count, keying position and connector body shape. Move to the mounting points, the pipe or hose stubs, and any bracket. Finish with incidental evidence: the work surface, the lighting, the dust pattern and the angle of the shadow.

The last step is not decorative. Two images taken minutes apart on one bench will share a background texture; two images drawn from a library will not. If the surfaces differ, the safest assumption is that the images show different items, and the supplier should be asked to confirm. A buyer who overrides that instinct because the numbers "nearly match" is taking a gamble with the return freight.

  1. Confirm the component type in both images before reading any number.
  2. Compare connector keying and pin count; these are the fastest discriminators.
  3. Compare mounting points and port positions, including left and right orientation.
  4. Check that the background and lighting are consistent with one photo session.
  5. Read the marking only after the visual comparison supports "same item."
  6. Log the result as confirmed, unresolved or blocked — never as "probably fine."

A resend request that gets an answer

Vague requests produce vague replies. "Please send clearer photos" gives the supplier no way to know what is missing. A good resend request names the enquiry row, restates the requested reference without changing it, lists exactly which views are required, and explains why. It also keeps the original request intact, so that a later change is visible as a change. The template below can be copied with the bracketed fields filled in.

Resend request template

"Reference row [your row identifier]: please keep our requested reference [XXXX-XXXXXX] unchanged on this line. The material supplied so far does not yet establish that the photographed item and the quoted item are the same. Please resend: (1) one wide, uncropped view of the complete item; (2) a straight-on, legible photograph of the physical marking, in focus with no reflection over the characters; (3) a photograph of that item beside its own packaging label; and (4) a wide view of the connector face showing pin count and keying. Please also state, in writing, which vehicle variant and identification basis you used for the quote. Until these are provided we will hold the line as unresolved and will not substitute the proposed number. Fitment must be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering."

Two details make this template work. First, it does not accuse anyone — it states that the evidence is insufficient, which is factual and easy to answer. Second, it asks for a small, specific set of views rather than "more photos." A supplier can satisfy four named requests in a single batch; an open-ended request invites the same ambiguous images again. If the supplier cannot provide a legible marking, ask them to say so explicitly rather than sending another angled photograph.

Keeping the original line while a proposal is reviewed

Once the resend arrives, there are two possible outcomes. Either the evidence supports the requested item and the line moves forward, or it supports a different reference. In the second case, the different reference becomes a proposal with its own line and its own status. The original request is never overwritten. This preserves the audit trail: a buyer can see what was asked, what was offered, what evidence arrived, and who accepted the change.

A market note belongs here too. What counts as sufficient evidence varies: a buyer in a market with strict documentation requirements may need the packaging label and the written specification before accepting a change, while another may accept a clear marking photograph. The workflow does not change — only the evidence threshold does. State your threshold in the RFQ so the supplier knows what to send the first time.

Questions buyers ask about photo and number conflicts

Which number is right when the part, the label and the quote disagree?

None of them can be assumed right. Record each as a separate observation, keep the line unresolved, and ask for the specific views listed in the resend template. The decision comes after the evidence, not before it.

Does a filename containing my reference prove the photo shows my part?

No. A filename is how a supplier organised a file. Ask for an uncropped view of the item and its marking instead, and compare the connector and mounting features.

Can I accept the number on a replacement quote if everything else looks the same?

Only after it is recorded as a separate proposal with a written identification basis. Keep the original reference unchanged and confirm the specification in writing before approving the change.

What if the supplier cannot provide a readable marking?

Ask them to say so in writing. An item with no legible marking can still be reviewed from the VIN, the connector layout and the packaging declaration, but it stays unconfirmed until those sources agree.

Do you guarantee that a photographed part fits my vehicle?

No. A photograph records appearance; it does not establish application. We confirm identity against the reference, VIN or label before accepting an order, and fitment must be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering.

How long does a conflict normally take to clear?

It depends on the supplier's response, not on the complexity of the part. A complete set of four named views usually resolves the line in one exchange; repeated angled or cropped photographs usually mean the supplier is working from a library rather than the actual item.
