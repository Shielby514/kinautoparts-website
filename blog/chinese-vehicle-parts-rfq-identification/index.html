Chinese Vehicle Parts RFQ Checklist by Brand and OE Reference

Published 12 November 2026 · KINP

For a Chinese-brand parts quotation, send the vehicle brand and model variant, the complete part reference, required quantity and destination. Add a clear part-label photograph when identification is uncertain. A catalogue result starts the enquiry; it does not confirm fitment or available stock.

Most parts enquiries do not fail at the pricing stage. They fail before that, in the first message, because the buyer supplied a brand logo, a model name and a blurry photograph, and expected the supplier to work out which physical part was wanted. A supplier can narrow a search from those clues, but cannot confirm an item from them, and a quotation built on an unconfirmed item is a guess with a number attached. This guide describes how to build a RFQ for Chinese-brand parts that a supplier can actually answer — one that separates what is known from what is assumed, and that treats a logo as a category label rather than as proof of anything.

The distinction between a catalogue result and a confirmed item is the backbone of the method. A catalogue result starts the conversation: it says that a reference exists for a brand and model group. It does not say that the reference fits the vehicle in front of you, that the supplier holds that item, or that the supplier has any relationship with the vehicle manufacturer. Keeping those three ideas separate — existence, application, and affiliation — prevents most of the disputes that follow a badly built enquiry.

Start with the brand, then the model variant

Chinese vehicle brands export under different names in different markets, and the same badge can cover more than one platform. Begin the RFQ by naming the brand and the model group exactly as it appears on the vehicle's identification plate or registration, then add the production year, engine code and market of destination. The sales name is a useful pointer, not an identifier: a name used in one region may map to a different platform in another, and the same platform may be sold under two names.

If the vehicle identification number is available, include it. It is the single most useful piece of evidence a supplier can be given, because it resolves mid-cycle changes that a model name cannot. If it is not available, say so explicitly and ask for identification review rather than letting the supplier assume. A RFQ that states what it does not know is more useful than one that appears complete and is quietly wrong. We would rather receive "brand, model, year, no VIN, label photo attached" than a confident model name with no evidence behind it.

The complete reference: copy every character

When the original reference is readable, copy it in full — letters, zeros, separators and version suffix. Do not reformat it, do not drop a leading zero, and do not shorten it to make the email look tidy. Chinese-brand references often carry a trailing segment that distinguishes a batch or a market variant; removing it changes the item being requested. If two references appear for one item on the old part or in a purchasing record, keep both and ask for review. Never choose between them on price or on which one looks more familiar.

Because we do not want illustrative numbers mistaken for real ones, the examples below use a placeholder format. The anatomy is what matters: a prefix that groups a family, a middle segment that usually points to the component and application, and an optional trailing segment that distinguishes a variant. A good RFQ is a short form with complete fields, not a long paragraph. The table below separates what is required for any identification review from what is recommended and what is merely helpful. Fill the required fields before sending; add the recommended ones when available; never invent a value to fill a blank. A field marked "not available" is more useful than a guessed one, because it tells the supplier which evidence route to use.

Notice that the symptom is listed as helpful, not required. A symptom is a clue about the system, not evidence about the part identity. It is useful for routing — a warm-cabin complaint belongs to the air-conditioning loop, a hum from a wheel belongs to the hub — but it must never be used to select a reference on its own. Enquiries that lead with the complaint and omit the reference are the ones that get quoted against the wrong item.

A brand logo is a category, not an authorization

This point causes more misunderstanding than any other. A brand logo in a catalogue identifies the vehicle-brand category of the parts listed beside it. It says that the parts are intended for that brand's vehicles. It does not say that the supplier was appointed by the manufacturer, that the supplier holds a distribution appointment, or that the items are original manufacturer components. Those are separate claims, and none of them follows from the presence of a logo.

The reason to be precise is practical. A buyer who assumes affiliation will make assumptions about specification, warranty and documentation that the supplier never offered. When the goods arrive, the disappointment is framed as a misrepresentation, when in fact the catalogue was doing only what catalogues do — grouping parts by brand. The correct language is "parts for [brand] vehicles," not "parts from [brand]." If affiliation or original-manufacturer supply matters to your market or warranty obligations, ask for the specific documents and record what is actually provided. Where the evidence is not provided, treat the claim as unconfirmed.

A VIN-and-label match takes you close to application confirmation. Only an explicit written statement supports a claim about what the supplier will do for this order.

Confirmed versus unconfirmed: label every line

Once the fields are filled, the buyer should mark every line with a status. This is the habit that turns a list of requests into a manageable document. A confirmed line is one where the reference has been tied to a label or a VIN and the supplier has restated the same item in writing. An unconfirmed line is anything else — a catalogue-only match, a partial number, a blurred label, or a supplier answer that changed the reference without explanation. Both are legitimate things to send; the error is treating the second as if it were the first.

  1. Write the requested line first and do not edit it later. The original reference and vehicle details are the baseline. If they change, create a new line rather than overwrite the old one.
  2. Attach the evidence to the line, not to the email. Put the label photo or VIN beside the specific item it supports. A general attachment folder invites mis-association.
  3. Mark catalogue-only matches unconfirmed. Existence in a catalogue is a starting point, not a decision.
  4. Record any supplier-proposed change as a separate proposal. Note the evidence offered and who reviewed it. Do not merge it into the requested line.
  5. Confirm fitment at the order boundary. State that fitment must be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering, and obtain acknowledgement.
  6. Close the loop in writing. The quote, the purchase order and the packing note should describe the same item under the same status label.

A status label is not bureaucracy. It is what lets a buyer look at a fifty-line RFQ and see instantly which twelve lines are safe to quote and which thirty-eight need a label photo. Without it, every line carries the same unearned confidence.

Market and variant differences that change the reference

Chinese brands export widely, and the same model name can cover different engine options, emission packages and running gear depending on destination. That is why market belongs in the required-fields list rather than the helpful one. A reference correct for a vehicle registered in one region can be wrong for the same badge elsewhere, and a suffix change in the middle of a production run can split what looks like one continuous model year into two part populations.

The practical response is not to memorise market rules; it is to supply the evidence that reveals them. VIN, engine code and production date do the work that a market rule would otherwise do. When those are unavailable, ask the supplier to state which variant they assumed and to flag the assumption in the quote. A supplier who writes "assumed based on model name" has given you something useful — a visible uncertainty you can resolve — whereas a silent assumption gives you a part that fails at fitment.

Questions buyers ask about building a RFQ

Do I need the VIN, or is the model name enough?

The model name is enough to open an enquiry and not enough to confirm an item. Include the VIN when you can. When you cannot, send the production year, engine code, market and a label photo, and ask for identification review.

What if the reference on the old part is partly unreadable?

Copy the part you can read, mark it unconfirmed, and send a close photograph of the label plus a wide photograph of the part in position. Partial evidence narrows a search; it does not confirm a fitment.

Can I send one RFQ with two different references for the same item?

Yes, and you should. Keep both lines, note that they relate to the same requested item, and ask which is correct for the VIN. Do not choose between them on price.

Does a supplier showing the brand logo mean they are appointed by the manufacturer?

No. A logo identifies a vehicle-brand category. It does not establish appointment, affiliation or original-manufacturer supply. Ask for specific documents if that matters to your order, and record what is actually provided.

What is the difference between a confirmed and an unconfirmed line?

A confirmed line ties the reference to a label or VIN and the supplier has restated the same item in writing. An unconfirmed line rests on a catalogue match, a partial number or an unverified assumption. Both may be quoted; only the first should be approved without further review.

Can you guarantee the parts will fit my vehicle?

We confirm identity against the reference, VIN or label before accepting an order, and we ask that fitment be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering. No catalogue entry or photograph alone establishes application.
