Comparing Two Quotations for the Same Chery Reference

Published 24 November 2026 · KINP

Two suppliers can quote the same Chery reference at very different prices without either quote being wrong. Before you compare the numbers, check that both offers describe the same physical item under the same conditions: the complete reference, the vehicle variant, the quoted specification, the quantity basis and the delivery terms. A price is only meaningful once those fields match.

Two suppliers can quote the same Chery reference at prices that differ by a third, and neither quote need be wrong. What usually differs is not the part but the conditions wrapped around it: one line includes packing, the other does not; one was priced at a low quantity for a distant port, the other at a family minimum; one describes the requested reference while the other quietly substitutes a nearby number. Comparing the two unit prices before those fields are aligned produces a decision that looks decisive and is actually arbitrary. This guide sets out the line-by-line method we use to make two offers genuinely comparable.

The method is deliberately unglamorous because it has to survive an audit. When a purchasing decision is questioned six months later — a batch fails, a warranty claim lands, a finance team asks why the higher price was approved — the only defensible answer is a sheet that shows what was compared, on what date, under which stated specification. A cheap quote that cannot be reconstructed is not a saving; it is an unrecorded risk. Everything below is about building that reconstructable sheet before the order, not after the problem.

Lock the item before you lock the price

A quotation describes one item under a set of conditions. If the item itself is not fixed, every other field is noise. The first request to each supplier is therefore not "what is your best price" but "restate the exact item you are pricing, and the basis you used to identify it." There are three legitimate bases: the complete OE reference read from the original label, the vehicle identification details (VIN, engine code, production date), or a supplier catalogue listing. They are not equal. A label or VIN basis narrows the item to a physical part; a catalogue listing only narrows it to a group.

Ask each supplier to state which of the three they used, and to write the full reference exactly — leading letters, zeros and version suffix included. A shortened number is a warning sign, not a convenience. For Chery references in particular, a trailing segment such as AA or BA can distinguish a variant, so dropping it changes what is being quoted. Where a supplier cannot say which variant was priced, mark the line unconfirmed and keep it out of the like-for-like comparison. An unconfirmed line is not the cheaper option; it is an unfinished sentence.

The fields that must sit on the same row

Once the item is fixed, align the fields that change the delivered cost. The discipline is physical: put the two offers into one sheet with identical column headings and make the supplier fill the blanks rather than letting them choose which fields to mention. A price that excludes a field is not lower; it is incomplete. The table below is the minimum set we use. Anything a supplier declines to fill stays blank on the sheet and is flagged, not guessed.

Delivered cost: what a unit price leaves out

The most common comparison error is treating the unit price as the cost. For export parts, the unit price is often the smaller half of what lands at the destination port. Freight, packing, documentation and the cost of a failure all sit outside it. A useful comparison therefore has two totals: the unit price as quoted, and an indicative landed cost that adds the fields the supplier did not include. The second number is not a promise — it is a planning figure that makes the two offers speak the same language.

The last row is where theory meets the workshop. This is why the comparison sheet keeps identification evidence beside the commercial fields: the cost of a wrong reference is real, even though it never appears on an invoice.

Keep a proposed alternative in its own column

The most consequential difference between two quotes is often hidden in a single line: one supplier has offered a different reference in place of the one requested. That is not automatically bad — alternatives are a normal part of sourcing when an original number has been superseded — but it must never be silently folded into the like-for-like price. A proposed alternative is a proposal, and it belongs in its own column until the specification and identity are confirmed in writing.

When a supplier proposes a change, ask four questions and record the answers verbatim. What evidence supports the change — dimensions, mounting points, connector keying, or a catalogue note? Who reviewed it, and are they named? Is the offered item built to the same stated specification as the requested one? And does the supplier accept that fitment must be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering, regardless of the alternative? A proposal that survives those four questions can move to the accepted list. One that does not stays where it is.

Build a comparison sheet your finance team can audit

The comparison sheet is the deliverable of this whole process. It need not be elaborate; it needs to be complete and dated. The following sequence turns two dissimilar emails into one defensible record. It works the same way for two suppliers or six.

  1. Create one row per requested line, not per supplier. The requested reference, vehicle details, quantity and destination sit at the head of the row. Supplier answers sit in aligned columns beneath it.
  2. Send a blank template to every supplier. Ask them to fill the field list in the same order. Do not let a supplier reply with a prose paragraph — prose cannot be compared cell by cell.
  3. Write the issue date on every returned quote. A price is a planning number tied to a period. Without the date, a later decision cannot be traced to the conditions it was made under.
  4. Mark every blank explicitly. Use "not stated" rather than leaving a cell empty. An empty cell invites someone to assume a value that was never offered.
  5. Separate accepted candidates from proposals. Colour, tab or label the two groups differently so nobody can approve a substituted line by accident.
  6. Record the decision and its reason in one sentence. For example: approved the higher unit price because it was the only line tied to the original label and included freight to the named port. That sentence is what survives an audit.

Done properly, the sheet answers three questions at once: what was compared, under which stated specification, and on what date. That is the difference between buying at a price and buying on a record.

When a quote is indicative, and how long it stays that way

Buyers sometimes treat an indicative price as a commitment and are surprised when it moves. It helps to be explicit about what an indicative figure is: a planning number built on the stated quantity, specification and cost conditions at the date shown, subject to confirmation when the order is placed. It is useful for budgeting and comparison and it is not a fixed offer. Market differences feed into it too — a destination with longer freight, stricter documentation or high local failure rates changes the delivered picture even when the unit price is identical.

This is also why the comparison sheet asks for the quote validity date as a field. Two quotes valid for different periods are not comparable in a rising-cost environment, and a quote that has expired should be re-requested rather than reused. We state indicative price bands with a date and a destination for exactly this reason, and we ask buyers to do the same when they circulate internal approvals. A number without a date will outlive the conditions that produced it.

Questions buyers ask when two quotes disagree

Which quote is cheaper once everything is included?

You cannot tell from the unit prices. Add the fields the supplier left out — packing, freight to your named port, documentation, warranty — and compare the indicative landed totals. In many cases the ordering reverses.

Can I treat two quotes with the same short number as the same item?

No. Ask both suppliers for the full reference and the identification basis. A dropped suffix or a catalogue-only basis means the line is unconfirmed and should be excluded from the like-for-like comparison.

A supplier offered a different reference at a better price. Should I take it?

Record it in its own column and ask for the supporting evidence and who reviewed it. An alternative is acceptable only when the specification is confirmed in writing and fitment is confirmed against VIN/label before ordering.

Why did the price change between the quote and the invoice?

Usually because the validity date expired, the quantity tier changed, or a specification field was filled in differently at order stage. A dated sheet with all fields completed makes the cause visible instead of disputed.

Do you guarantee the part will fit my vehicle?

No. We confirm identity against the reference, VIN or label before the order is accepted, and we ask that fitment be confirmed against VIN/label before ordering. A catalogue listing starts the enquiry; it does not establish application.

What is the fastest way to get comparable quotes from your side?

Send the complete reference or the VIN, the quantity, the destination port and the stated specification you require. We return a dated quote with packing, lead time, freight and validity written into the same fields, so it can be compared cell by cell with any other offer.
