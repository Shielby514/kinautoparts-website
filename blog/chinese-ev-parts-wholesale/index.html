On an electric vehicle, the system comes before the part number

A fleet buyer sends one message containing three requests: a left front control arm for a Chinese electric platform, an electric compressor for a second vehicle of the same marque, and a door latch for a third. On a combustion car those three lines might be handled independently. On an electric platform they cannot be, because the same marque can cover several platforms, the suspension pieces are handed and platform-specific, the compressor sits in a high-voltage circuit, and the latch belongs to a body harness with its own generation. Treating the three requests as one "parts list" is how a clean enquiry becomes three contested lines.

The catalogue behind this guide holds forty references across three systems. Cooling System accounts for twenty-three, Suspension & Steering twelve, and Body Parts five. The distribution is useful for planning a first enquiry but dangerous if it is read as a usage ranking. Cooling dominates because this range includes several generations of electric compressors, condensers, evaporators and water pumps; it does not mean that every vehicle needs a cooling part. The right starting question is always which system is being bought and why, followed by the platform, year and market.

This guide sets out a system-first workflow. It covers the suspension references and the electronic control items in the cooling group, and it places a safety gate in front of anything connected to the high-voltage system. Every OE number used below is taken from the catalogue table for this range. No number here is a fitment promise, and no statement here replaces the vehicle maker's service procedure. Fitment must be confirmed against the VIN or the label on the original part before ordering.

The safety gate before any high-voltage part is handled

Electric compressors and electric water pumps in this range are not ordinary service parts. They sit in circuits that carry voltages far above anything a workshop meets on a twelve-volt system, and they require isolation, documentation and transport review before they are removed, packed or returned. The sequence below is a procurement discipline as much as a technical one: no line is quoted as a like-for-like item until its high-voltage status is known.

  1. Step 1 — classify the line before pricing it. Ask explicitly whether the item is a service part, a remanufactured unit or a salvage-derived component, and request the handling documents that apply. These categories must not be treated as interchangeable because one catalogue lists them under the same vehicle.
  2. Step 2 — apply the vehicle maker's isolation procedure. Follow the published procedure for the platform, including the specified wait after the high-voltage system is disconnected. Many workshops use a minimum of ten minutes as a working rule, but the maker's procedure governs. Do not shorten it to meet a delivery date.
  3. Step 3 — verify the circuit is de-energised. Use a meter rated for the category of measurement involved and confirm zero voltage before touching a connector. Treat every orange-cabled component as live until that check is complete.
  4. Step 4 — record the label, not just the shape. Photograph the part label with the voltage and reference visible, then photograph the connector with the cavity count and keying position. A compressor that looks identical to another may belong to a different generation.
  5. Step 5 — keep high-voltage items out of a general carton plan. Ask for the applicable transport documents and packing requirements for the destination. Mixing these items into an ordinary parts shipment can hold the whole consignment at the port.
  6. Step 6 — require qualified handling at both ends. Components that affect braking, steering, airbags or high-voltage systems should be reviewed by a qualified technician before a replacement is accepted. Catalogue data can identify a candidate; it cannot replace physical inspection and vehicle-specific verification.

If any of those steps cannot be completed, the line stays pending. An incomplete high-voltage line is not a commercial inconvenience; it is a reason to stop. This is also why the catalogue's body and suspension lines are kept in separate columns from the cooling group in our enquiries: the evidence they require is different in kind.

Electronic control: why one prefix can cover different behaviour

Modules and electrically driven assemblies carry a further variable beyond the part number: the hardware or software generation fitted during a production run. Two compressors with references in the same series can behave differently across model years, and a water pump fitted to one platform may use a different control interface from the pump on a related platform. The catalogue reflects this with multiple lines rather than one catch-all reference. The correct buyer response is to add the build date and the connector record to every electrical line.

A practical rule follows from this table: a reference is a candidate until the interface is matched. For mechanical wear items the interface is a mounting point and a dimension; for electronic items it is also a connector and a generation. If the evidence is incomplete, keep the line pending rather than approving it to meet a shipping date. That rule is unpopular when a container is closing, and it is far cheaper than the alternative.

Market and duty cycle determine the first order, not the catalogue count

The catalogue's distribution tells you where references exist; the destination tells you which ones to ask about first. In hot markets, cooling lines dominate: electric compressors, condensers and evaporators work continuously, and a vehicle parked in the sun after a long drive keeps the system under load. The motor radiator 1466286400 and the external heat exchanger 1317784900 tend to appear in those enquiries. In markets with rough roads and high urban mileage, the suspension group moves instead: control arms, H-arms, hub units and stabilizer links are the lines buyers remember. Cold regions add a different emphasis, where coolant circulation, the auxiliary tanks 1259829000 and 1342597200, and the heater side of the cabin system come into the conversation. These are planning observations, not measured wear rates, and they never override the build in front of you.

Do vehicles under the same Chinese brand share suspension parts?

Not automatically. The platform, not the marque, decides. 202001971AA and 202001972AA are the left and right front control arms in this catalogue, while 202002395AA and 202002396AA are a separate H-arm pair. Confirm the VIN and the axle before naming either.

Is an electric water pump an ordinary cooling part?

It is listed under Cooling System, but 302001909AA and 1497320000 must be classified before they are handled. Establish whether the unit is in the high-voltage circuit, obtain the handling documents and follow the vehicle maker's isolation procedure. Do not treat it as a like-for-like item on the strength of its shape.

Why are there so many electric compressor references?

Because generations and interfaces differ. 1320043000, 1370835100, 1461039100, 1502970200 and 1357158800 are separate lines in this table. Send the build date, the label and the connector record; the alternative is guessing from a photograph.

What should a first suspension order contain?

Choose lines you can verify: one handed pair with the side recorded, and a hub unit or link with a clear part photograph. Keep quantities per reference. A small verified order teaches you more about the platform than a large order built on assumptions.

Can a high-voltage item be added to an existing consolidated shipment?

Only after its documents and packing requirements are known. Plan it as a separate line from the beginning. Adding it late can hold the whole consignment at the port, and the delay costs more than the freight saving.

Are these forty references parts you can deliver immediately?

No. They are an identification list for sourcing enquiries. Availability, documentation, lead time and pricing are established against the specific order and destination. Nothing in this guide is a stock or delivery undertaking.
